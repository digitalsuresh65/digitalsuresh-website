A digital marketing plan should help you decide what to do, what not to do, and how you will learn from the work. It does not need to be a long document. It needs to connect your business goal to a specific audience, a clear message, and a manageable set of actions.

Use the following structure as a practical starting point.

## 1. Define the business goal

Begin with the business outcome your marketing should support. “Post more on social media” is an activity, not a business goal. A stronger goal might be to increase suitable enquiries for a particular service, introduce an offer to a new audience, or improve repeat business from existing customers.

Write down:

- the outcome you want to influence;
- why it matters now;
- the period you will review; and
- any important limits, such as budget, capacity, or geography.

Avoid setting a goal your team cannot support operationally. More leads are not useful if nobody can respond or deliver the service.

## 2. Choose the audience you most need to reach

Broad marketing often becomes vague marketing. Define the group most relevant to the goal. Focus on the situation they are in, the problem they need to solve, and what affects their decision.

Useful questions include:

- What has happened that makes this problem important now?
- What are they trying to achieve?
- What concerns could stop them from acting?
- What information would help them compare options?
- Where do they look for advice or providers?

Use customer conversations and real enquiry language wherever possible. Assumptions can be useful starting points, but label them as assumptions until you can test them.

## 3. Clarify the offer and message

Your audience should be able to understand what you help with, who it is for, and what the next step is. Write a simple message before creating campaigns or content.

One useful structure is:

> We help [specific audience] address [important problem] through [relevant approach], so they can work toward [intended benefit].

This is not necessarily the final wording for your website. It is a decision tool that keeps your marketing aligned.

Then decide what evidence, explanation, or demonstration a prospect needs before they will feel comfortable taking the next step. Do not invent social proof. Use verified information, clear process explanations, useful samples, or transparent answers to common questions.

## 4. Map the customer journey

People rarely move from first awareness to a sales conversation in one step. Map the questions they may have at three simple stages:

1. **Awareness:** Do they recognize the problem and know your business exists?
2. **Consideration:** Do they understand your approach and whether it fits their situation?
3. **Action:** Is the next step clear, relevant, and easy to complete?

Review your current website, content, ads, email, and follow-up against those stages. Look for missing information or unnecessary friction.

## 5. Select a small number of channels

Choose channels based on audience behavior and the job each channel needs to do. Search may capture existing demand. Social content may build familiarity. Email may support follow-up. Paid advertising may help reach a defined audience more quickly.

Using every channel is rarely the best starting point. Choose a manageable combination and define the role of each one.

For example:

- a search-focused service page to answer high-intent questions;
- a monthly article that addresses a key customer concern;
- short social posts that introduce the article’s main ideas; and
- a short email sequence that follows up after an enquiry or download.

## 6. Build an action plan

Turn the strategy into work with clear ownership. For every priority, record:

- the action;
- the person responsible;
- the information or asset needed;
- the intended completion point; and
- the signal you will review.

Keep the first plan realistic. A smaller plan that is consistently implemented will teach you more than an ambitious plan that stalls.

## 7. Decide how you will learn

Measurement should help you make a decision. Choose signals connected to the goal, such as qualified enquiries, consultation requests, conversion rate, cost per relevant lead, or response rate. Supporting signals such as page visits or content engagement can help explain what happened, but they should not automatically become the main goal.

Schedule a regular review. Ask:

- What happened?
- What might explain it?
- What did we learn from customers or sales conversations?
- What will we keep, change, stop, or test next?

A marketing plan is not a prediction. It is a coordinated set of choices and a way to learn. Keep it clear enough that the people doing the work can use it.
